Lecture 16: Foundations of Data Visualization

Why visualization works, how people perceive charts, and how to choose the right visual for the analytical question you are answering.

1. Learning Objectives

  • 1 Explain why visualization is more effective than tables for pattern discovery and communication.
  • 2 Apply basic principles of visual perception (pre-attentive attributes, Gestalt) to chart design.
  • 3 Map common analytical questions to appropriate chart families.
  • 4 Identify and avoid the most common visualization mistakes.
  • 5 Prepare a simple “chart choice” checklist for your own projects.

2. Motivation

A well-chosen chart can reveal a trend, outlier, or comparison in seconds. A poor chart can hide the truth or invent a false story. Visualization is not decoration — it is part of the analytical method and the communication method.

💡 Key Insight
The goal is not “pretty charts.” The goal is accurate, fast understanding that supports a decision.

3. Real-World Story

The Pie Chart That Hid a Crisis

A operations team presented monthly defect rates as a colorful pie chart of “share of defects by type.” Leadership concluded the mix was stable. When an analyst re-drew the same data as a line chart of defect count over 12 months, a clear upward trend appeared in one category — the real problem that needed action.

Same data. Wrong visual. Wrong conclusion. This lecture teaches you to match form to purpose.

4. Why Visualize?

  • Pattern detection — trends, seasonality, clusters, outliers are faster in pictures than in tables.
  • Comparison — magnitudes and rankings are easier with aligned bars or positions.
  • Communication — stakeholders absorb a clear visual faster than a paragraph of numbers.
  • Exploration — interactive visuals let you slice and drill without writing a new query each time.

5. Perception Basics

Humans decode some visual properties almost instantly (pre-attentive attributes):

  • Position on a common scale
  • Length
  • Angle / slope
  • Color hue and intensity (use carefully)
  • Size / area (weaker; people misjudge areas)

Position and length are the most accurate channels for quantitative comparison. Prefer them for the most important numbers.

Gestalt ideas (practical)
  • Proximity — items close together are seen as a group.
  • Similarity — same color/shape suggests same category.
  • Enclosure — borders and cards create “this belongs together.”
  • Connection — lines link related points (e.g., time series).

Website / course data tip
When visualizing course progress, website traffic, or learning analytics: start with the decision (e.g., “which module needs more examples?”), not with a pie of page views.

Geographic patternMap (choropleth / symbol)Bar of every city when map is clearer
RankingSorted bar chartUnsorted bars; pie

8. Common Mistakes

Avoid
  • 3D effects and heavy decoration
  • Truncated y-axes that exaggerate differences
  • Too many colors or categories
  • Pie charts with 8+ slices
  • Dual axes that invent false correlations
  • Missing units, titles, or data source
Prefer
  • Clear title that states the insight or question
  • Direct labeling when possible
  • Consistent color meaning across a dashboard
  • Sorted bars for rankings
  • One main message per chart
  • Accessible contrast (color-blind safe palettes)

9. Practical

Choose the Chart

For each need, pick a chart type and one reason:

  1. Compare revenue of 12 product categories this quarter
  2. Show monthly active users for the last 24 months
  3. Show how defect types share of total defects (5 types)
  4. Explore whether order value and delivery days are related
  5. Rank top 10 sales reps by closed deals
Sample answers +
  1. Horizontal bar (many categories, easy to read labels)
  2. Line chart (time series)
  3. Stacked bar or simple donut (few parts)
  4. Scatter plot
  5. Sorted horizontal bar

11. Summary

  • Visualization is for accurate, fast understanding — not decoration.
  • Prefer position and length for quantitative comparisons.
  • Match chart type to the analytical question (comparison, time, distribution, relationship, part-to-whole).
  • Avoid 3D, truncated axes, and overloaded pies.
