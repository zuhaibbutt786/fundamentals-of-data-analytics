Lecture 12: Correlation & Relationships

Pearson & Spearman correlation, multicollinearity & VIF, and a practical guide to choosing the right statistical test — fully aligned with the CS-399 outline and the “Choosing the Right Statistical Test” slides.

1. Learning Objectives

  • 1 Compute and interpret Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients.
  • 2 Build and read a correlation matrix / heatmap.
  • 3 Detect multicollinearity with VIF and decide what to do about it.
  • 4 Choose the appropriate statistical test based on variable type and research question.
  • 5 Apply the decision framework to real business / healthcare / education scenarios.

2. Motivation

Almost every analytical question eventually asks: “Are these two things related?” or “Is there a difference between groups?” Choosing the wrong test produces either meaningless p-values or, worse, false confidence. This lecture gives you the mental map that professional analysts use every day.

3. Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r)

Measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two continuous numerical variables.

\[ r = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} \]

  • \( r = 1 \): perfect positive linear relationship
  • \( r = -1 \): perfect negative linear relationship
  • \( r = 0 \): no linear relationship (there may still be a non-linear one)
Rule of thumb (approximate)
|r| < 0.3 weak · 0.3–0.7 moderate · > 0.7 strong (always plot the data!)

4. Spearman Rank Correlation (ρ)

Used when data are ranked (ordinal) or when the relationship is monotonic but not strictly linear.

\[ \rho = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)} \]

where \( d_i \) is the difference between the ranks of each pair and \( n \) is the number of observations.

When to prefer Spearman: outliers, ordinal scales, or clear non-linear but still monotonic patterns.

5. Multicollinearity & VIF

Multicollinearity occurs when two or more independent variables in a regression model are highly correlated. It inflates standard errors and makes coefficient estimates unstable.

\[ \text{VIF} = \frac{1}{1 - R^2} \]

where \( R^2 \) comes from regressing one predictor on all the other predictors.

  • VIF = 1 → no multicollinearity
  • VIF 5–10 → moderate concern
  • VIF > 10 → serious problem (consider removing or combining variables)

6. Choosing the Right Statistical Test

This section is adapted from the course slide deck “Choosing the Right Statistical Test” by Zuhaib Hussain Butt. The correct test depends on two things: the type of variables you have and the question you want to answer.

6.1 Type of Variables

TypeSub-typeExamples
CategoricalNominalGender, Department, Blood type
OrdinalRating (Poor → Excellent), Education level
QuantitativeContinuousHeight, Weight, Age, Salary
Discrete (counts)Number of children, number of claims

6.2 Decision Framework

ObjectiveVariable typesRecommended test(s)
Compare means of two independent groups 1 categorical (2 levels) + 1 continuous Independent Samples t-test (normal)
Mann-Whitney U (non-normal)
Compare means of paired / repeated measures Same subjects measured twice Paired t-test (normal)
Wilcoxon signed-rank (non-normal)
Compare means of three or more groups 1 categorical (≥3 levels) + 1 continuous One-way ANOVA (normal)
Kruskal-Wallis (non-normal)
Association between two categorical variables Both categorical Chi-square test of independence
Linear relationship between two continuous variables Both continuous Pearson correlation (normal / linear)
Spearman correlation (monotonic / ranks)
Predict continuous outcome from several predictors Multiple continuous / mixed Multiple linear regression
Predict binary outcome Binary dependent + predictors Logistic regression
Count data outcome Count dependent Poisson regression (or Negative Binomial)

6.3 Practical Decision Steps

  1. Identify your variables – categorical or quantitative? nominal / ordinal / continuous / discrete?
  2. Check the distribution – for quantitative variables use histograms, Q-Q plots or Shapiro-Wilk. This decides parametric vs non-parametric.
  3. State the objective clearly – comparing groups? testing association? modelling a relationship?
  4. Select the test from the table above.
  5. Check assumptions and report effect size, not only p-value.

7. Worked Examples (from the slides)

Example 1 – Weight loss across diets

Variables: Diet (Keto / Mediterranean / Vegan) + Weight loss (kg)
Test: ANOVA (if normal) or Kruskal-Wallis (if not)

Example 2 – Gender × Department

Variables: Gender + Department (both categorical)
Test: Chi-square test of independence

Example 3 – Age vs Spending

Variables: Age + Spending (both continuous)
Test: Pearson (linear + normal) or Spearman (otherwise)

Example 4 – Two teaching methods

Variables: Method A vs B + Test scores
Test: Independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U

8. Python Practical

Use sample_sales.csv or load Titanic / Gapminder from the public links in the datasets README.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

df = pd.read_csv("../../datasets/sample_sales.csv")

# Pearson & Spearman between Quantity and UnitPrice
print("Pearson :", df["Quantity"].corr(df["UnitPrice"]))
print("Spearman:", df["Quantity"].corr(df["UnitPrice"], method="spearman"))

# Correlation matrix + heatmap
num = df[["Quantity","UnitPrice","Discount"]]
corr = num.corr()
sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, cmap="coolwarm", center=0)
plt.title("Correlation Matrix")
plt.show()

12. Flashcards

Pearson vs Spearman?
Pearson = linear continuous; Spearman = ranks / monotonic
VIF formula?
1 / (1 − R²) from regressing one predictor on the others
Two categorical vars?
Chi-square test of independence
Two groups, continuous outcome?
t-test (normal) or Mann-Whitney U (non-normal)

13. Cheat Sheet – Choosing the Test

  • 2 groups, continuous → t-test / Mann-Whitney
  • Paired continuous → Paired t / Wilcoxon
  • ≥3 groups, continuous → ANOVA / Kruskal-Wallis
  • 2 categorical → Chi-square
  • 2 continuous → Pearson / Spearman
  • Always check normality & report effect size

14. Summary

  • Pearson for linear continuous relationships; Spearman for ranks or monotonic patterns.
  • VIF diagnoses multicollinearity — values > 10 are a red flag.
  • Choosing the right test starts with variable type + research objective (see the decision table).
  • Always visualise first and report practical significance, not only p-values.
